# Third-party licensing plan

> Technical planning, not legal advice.

## The entry rule

No tool and no prebuilt binary enters the product without:

- the upstream project and its URL;
- the exact version or tag, and the exact artifact;
- a SHA-256 and, where one exists, a signature check;
- the licence of both the source and the artifact being distributed;
- the list of transitive components and their notices;
- instructions for the source offer where one is required;
- a recorded decision about the relevant codecs and patents.

## What ships today

Nine tools travel inside the installer, and every one of them is permissive — MIT,
Apache-2.0, BSD-2 or Unlicense. That is deliberate: **nothing copyleft is in the
installer.** It costs nothing here, because the GPL tools are the large ones and were
headed for the download channel regardless.

Nine more are pinned as downloadable and fetched by the app. `vendor/THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.txt`
and `vendor/tool-inventory.json` are generated by the build, listing name, version,
licence, origin, artifact URL and hash for everything bundled.

## FFmpeg

FFmpeg is LGPL-2.1-or-later by default, but GPL options pull the whole of FFmpeg under
the GPL. The build we distribute has to be ours, or come from a supplier whose
configuration and corresponding sources are verifiable. The conservative route is to
avoid `--enable-gpl` and `--enable-nonfree`, follow the project's LGPL checklist, and
document which codecs stop being available.

The pinned artifact is BtbN's `win64-lgpl` build from a dated, immutable tag. The project
publishes no official Windows binary of its own, which is exactly why the supplier and
the variant are recorded rather than assumed.

## yt-dlp

The repository is Unlicense, but the packaged executables can contain components under
other licences — the upstream README warns that PyInstaller builds may be GPLv3+. So
"yt-dlp is Unlicense" is not enough: the chosen artifact and its third-party file need an
audit. Full YouTube support also needs an external JS runtime; Deno is the recommended
option and is installed alongside it.

## PDF and images

qpdf is Apache-2.0 and covers structural transformation, not rendering. Poppler is
GPL-2.0-only or GPL-3.0-only, invoked as a separate process and shipped only through the
download channel. PDFium uses a BSD-style licence in its core and carries transitive
notices. libvips is LGPL-2.1-or-later. Those facts make the combination possible, not
automatically compatible: the exact Windows package still needs its inventory.

## Rejected on licensing grounds

- **Ghostscript** — AGPL-3.0, with Artifex enforcing it against distribution alongside
  non-AGPL software. It would enter only if ToolHaven itself became AGPL.
- **pngquant** — GPL-3.0-or-later, with a commercial licence offered explicitly for
  non-GPL applications. Even across a process boundary, upstream frames that use as a
  commercial case, which is precisely the ambiguity this document exists to avoid.

## Pipeline outputs

- `THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.txt` for the core, plus notices for each on-demand package,
  reachable from the About screen before and after installation.
- `sbom.cdx.json` in the release.
- `tool-inventory.json` showing name, version, licence and site.
- Corresponding source files or offers for the LGPL and GPL components.
- A configuration and difference report for any build we produce ourselves.

## The decision that changes everything

The application's own licence is MIT and the repository is public. A permissive portfolio
app and a closed commercial product have different tolerances for copyleft dependencies.
That is why the installer stays permissive-only and every copyleft tool is a separate
process fetched on demand.
